# The Affordable Home OCMEE: Ready, Together, Give

Three one-pagers that tell one story. The Ramos family, Frank and Bright Supply Co. are **made up for this demo**. Every number has a source or is labelled ASSUMPTION. Checked 7 November 2026.

---

## 1. Ready: a family finds its way in

**The problem.** A typical Orange County house sold for $1,470,000, and C.A.R. puts the income needed to buy it at $374,400 a year. A working family of four earns a fraction of that. *(C.A.R. Housing Affordability Index, Orange County row.)*

**Meet the Ramos family (made up).** Ana and Luis Ramos live in Santa Ana with two kids. Together they earn $118,000 a year (ASSUMPTION). They rent a 2-bed for about $2,700 a month (ASSUMPTION, close to Zillow's typical Santa Ana rent).

**What renting costs them.** At $2,700 a month, rising 3.5% a year (ASSUMPTION), 30 years of rent comes to about **$1.67 million**. At the end, they own nothing.

**What they could pay.** At 30% of their gross income, $2,950 a month covers mortgage, tax and insurance (ASSUMPTION for this family). At today's 7.18% rate that supports a price of about **$360,000**. *(Freddie Mac, 5 Nov 2026.)* Their $118,000 puts them in the **lower-income** group for four, under the $148,850 limit. *(HCD 2026.)*

**Where help comes in.** Ana's housing counselor opens the tool with her and picks Santa Ana. It finds:
- **Santa Ana My First Home:** up to $120,000 at 0%, no payments until they sell. They are under the $148,850 limit for four.
- **CalHFA MyHome:** 3% of the price, also deferred.

Deferred help has no monthly payment, so the Ramos family can now pay about **$479,000** with the same monthly cost.

The Ready tab also checks Habitat for Humanity of OC: their income fits the $55,850 to $166,300 range for four, their $500 of monthly debts is 5.1% of income (Habitat needs under 10%), and as a lower-income family they would owe 250 sweat-equity hours per adult on a new home.

**What she gets is a plan, not a promise.** The tool marks every rule it could not confirm, such as whether the two programs combine. It says plainly: early check only; the program staff decide.

**Real today:** the Ready tab: income group, program fit with reasons, Habitat check and next steps. **Next:** Spanish and Vietnamese screens.

## 2. Together: a city keeps its workers close

**The problem.** The people who teach, nurse and cook in a city often cannot live in it. We do not have a sourced commute figure for Orange County workers, so we do not quote one.

**The gap for the builder.** The lane's example home costs **$535,000** to deliver (ASSUMPTION, lane 3 synthetic deal). A nonprofit must price it for the income group, not the family: California caps the cost for a lower-income family of four at 30% of 70% of the area median, **$2,425 a month**, or about **$296,000** at 7.18%. *(H&SC 50052.5; HCD 2026 median $138,600.)* That leaves a **$239,000 gap per home** before any help. Santa Ana's deferred loan alone would close about $105,000 of it.

**What the city can do, without naming anyone.**
- **See demand as counts only.** "40 families in our city are nearly ready" (illustration). No names leave anyone's device.
- **Offer land.** Land is the biggest line in the deal: $360,000 of the $535,000. In the Together tab, a city offering half the land free removes $180,000 from the gap. Ask each city what it can offer.
- **Speed things up.** In the lane's example, a 10-month slip cost about seven times more in fundraising. Every month of delay adds holding costs: about $7,083 a month in this example (ASSUMPTION). Faster permits are money.
- **Host Build Days.** Habitat for Humanity of OC asks 250 hours of help per adult on a new home for a low-income family, 100 on a resale and 25 for moderate income. A monthly city Build Day lets families earn those hours beside neighbors. *(habitatoc.org, 2026.)*

**Real today:** the gap calculator, city levers (land, fees, delay) and a Build Day planner. **Next:** a city view with counts, never names.

## 3. Give: neighbors who want to help

**Meet Frank (made up).** Frank retired from engineering. He has more than he needs and wants his money to do something he can see.

**What his gift does.** Frank asks: "What does $25,000 pay for?" The Give tab answers with the deal's own lines. On a home still $83,615 short after public funds and rent (lane assumptions), $25,000 closes about **30%** of it. Spread across the costs, it pays roughly $16,800 of land, $4,000 of holding costs, $1,900 of construction and $2,300 of fees. Every line is labelled ASSUMPTION or sourced.

**Bright Supply Co. (made up)** gives windows and lumber. Donated goods can be resold or used on site.

**Taxes, honestly.**
- Gifts to a 501(c)(3) charity are generally deductible for donors who itemize. *(IRS Publication 526.)*
- Donated goods are valued under IRS Publication 561. Non-cash gifts over $500 need Form 8283.
- A city cannot hand out income tax breaks. What cities can do is lower costs: fee deferrals, faster review and land. Ask each city; we did not confirm any.
- Donors should ask their own tax advisor.

**Giving is not investing.** A donation does not pay anyone back. Some community loan funds lend to affordable housing and pay a modest return, but that is an investment with risk and needs a licensed advisor. We keep the two apart.

**The ending.** With the Santa Ana loan, CalHFA's help, a city award and Frank's gift, the Ramos home is funded. Two years later, Luis leads a Build Day for the next family.

### How the three fit
**Ready** finds families before they apply. **Together** brings land, speed and people. **Give** brings money and materials. Because every number shows its source and date, another team's tool can plug into ours.

*Made-up demo people. Not legal, financial or lending advice. Early check only. Program staff make every final decision.*
